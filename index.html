How to Think Like a Creative (Without the Fairy Dust)

A Guide to Building Creativity Without Waiting for Inspiration

As a designer with a decade of working in the field, I've often heard peers talk about creativity like it’s magic. As if the muse will descend, bestow her blessings, and voilà! You have your idea.

Some prominent creatives even double down on that myth. In her book Big Magic, Elizabeth Gilbert shared a story about poet Ruth Stone, who described feeling a poem coming toward her “like a thunderous train of air.” When it did, she'd frantically scramble for a pencil and paper, hoping to write it down before it passed through her. If she made it in time, great. If not? The poem would keep barreling forward, looking for someone else.

To Gilbert, this wasn't just a metaphor. It was evidence. As she puts it, “I believe that creativity is a force of enchantment — not entirely human in its origins.” It is the idea that decides whether you're ready to receive it or not.

But if that's your strategy, and you're waiting to be struck by genius, you're going to be waiting a long time.

Creativity is entirely human, and that's what makes it so powerful. You don't need divine intervention to build something groundbreaking. You just need to look closely at how things work and how they could work better.

Case in point: the iPhone.

A 21st Century "Miracle"

When the iPhone launched in 2007, it felt otherworldly. A touchscreen device that could call, text, browse the web, play music, and fit in your pocket? It was hailed as revolutionary. Magical, even.

But look closer, and the magic starts to look like method.

Steve Jobs didn't invent the touchscreen. He didn't invent mobile internet. He didn't invent music players, phones, or even visual voicemail. What he and his team at Apple did was observe all those existing elements and ask: What happens if we combine them in a way that feels seamless?

The team borrowed from the Newton, the iPod, industrial design, and Pixar's focus on story and clarity. They noticed what people hated about mobile phones, like buttons, styluses, clunky interfaces, and rearranged the familiar into something new: a finger-based touch navigation system, combined with smarter software, designed for a better user experience. That's creativity. It's not summoning ideas from the ether, but making something transformative by seeing and reworking what's already there. “Creativity,” according to Jobs, “is just connecting things.”

And connecting things isn't reserved for geniuses or prodigies. It's built into how the human brain works.

Soap ≠ Toothpaste: A Lesson in Thinking

At its core, thinking consists of two actions: integration and differentiation. You notice something in the world, connect it to what you already know (integration), and distinguish it from what it's not (differentiation).

We do this from the moment we begin making sense of the world. When my daughter was about three, we had a nightly routine: I'd hand her the toothbrush so she could practice brushing her teeth. One night, I stepped away to grab her pajamas. When I came back, I tried to finish brushing for her, but she immediately covered her mouth and shouted, “No!”

Confused, I asked what was wrong. In her best toddler speak, she explained that she added hand soap instead of more toothpaste to the brush.

Her little brain had made a connection: Toothpaste and soap both clean things. That's integration. But after one try, she also learned they definitely don't taste the same. That's differentiation.

She had observed, connected, and adjusted her mental categories, which is exactly what thinking is meant to do. Over time, we continue refining and reshaping our mental models. The process just gets more complex as we age.

Thinking on the Job

As product designers, we do the same kind of cognitive work, just with more variables and higher stakes. We scan for patterns across platforms and behaviors, we integrate ideas across use cases, and we sharpen our thinking by differentiating what works from what doesn't.

Let's break down the process in a domain we know well: app onboarding.

If you've ever designed an onboarding flow, you know it's full of tough calls. How much should we explain before letting the user dive in? Which screens should be skippable? How much time can we ask for before someone bails?

These are the very questions Headspace likely faced when designing their current onboarding. They would have studied the market leaders of the time—Calm, Insight Timer, Simple Habit—and spotted what they had in common: long explanations of how meditation works, why it matters, and what you'll get out of it. While well-intentioned, these intros kept users reading and in a slightly impatient state.

Some patterns worked: a clear value prop, a friendly tone, and gentle guidance. And some patterns didn't work: too many words before any action, and a first experience that feels like homework. At the time, all other meditation apps started the experience with theory. Would Headspace do the same?

Or… was there a better way that made the app's value tangible early on? Instead of starting with theory, could they, instead, start with practice? What if the very first thing a user does is meditate?

And that's what Headspace did. When you first open the app, it immediately asks you to take 3 deep breaths. That simple action instantly puts users in a calmer state and makes them more likely to follow through. It was a novel idea shaped by the patterns they noticed, the conclusions they drew, and the emotional state they wanted their users to be in.

That's creativity: thinking in action, directed toward a new result.

That Magic Moment is More Human than You Think

But, wait! What about that random idea you had while in the shower? Perhaps you were happily humming along to your favorite song when… BAM! Suddenly, a great idea hits you, and you are left convinced that the muse finally paid a visit.

I hate to burst bubbles, but that wasn't a lightning bolt. It was your mind, silently working in the background. You may have been preoccupied, asleep, or in the shower, but your subconscious was busy sorting, stitching, and connecting all the thinking you did earlier. It felt sudden only because you weren't watching it happen.

But you did feed it. Your subconscious only made that creative leap because of the raw material you gave it when you thought about, wrestled with, and stored earlier observations. It's like building muscle. You train hard at the gym, but the actual growth happens during rest. If you hit the same muscle group too soon, you interrupt the repair process and stall your progress.

Your brain is no different. You gather input by paying attention, by noticing how things work, and by actively engaging with the world. That's your mental workout. But then you have to give your mind space to process it all, which is where the magic happens, through thoughtful fuel and strategic rest.

No innovation from the iPhone to Headspace happened due to a single, dazzling meeting. These products are formed by years of insights applied in smarter ways. And somewhere, maybe in the shower, those insights clicked. Not because of some muse, but because the team had done the work to earn the “aha.”

Creativity is a Skill

And if that sounds like it strips the wonder out of creativity, that's good news. If creativity is something you do, then it's something you can practice. It's a skill. And like any skill, it gets sharper the more you use it. The more curious you get, the more raw material you collect. And the more raw material you collect, the more creative you can become.

Creativity isn't magic, it's cognition. It's less like a lightning bolt and more like a lantern that you light and fuel yourself. So instead of waiting for inspiration to show up, ask: What can I make with what's already in front of me? That's how you make the next iPhone. That's how you create, and how you leave the world a little different than you found it.
